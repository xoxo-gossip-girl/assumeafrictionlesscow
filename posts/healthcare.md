### ‘What is the greatest healthcare challenge in the world at the moment, and what steps can we take to solve it?’

In 2025, children born in lower-income countries were 15 times more likely to die before the age of 5 compared to their counterparts born in wealthier countries despite the lifesaving medical technology existing elsewhere in the world. (WHO, 2025) The greatest healthcare challenge today is not innovation itself, but ensuring these breakthroughs reach everyone who needs them. Recently, scientific breakthroughs have enabled millions of lives to be saved. These new technologies range from the first successful trial of personalized gene editing to new drugs that can slow the spread of Huntington’s disease. However, many of these breakthroughs are centralised in higher income countries. (Rosen, 2025) Therefore, much of this technology is simply not accessible or feasible to communities around the world who live in middle- and lower-income countries. These communities, despite being equally in need of such technologies as their wealthier counterparts, are unable to access them. Currently, in low-income countries, government and donor spending on health averaged just $17 per capita, under half of the target of $60 per capita, which is the estimated required spending to provide essential healthcare services. (Group, 2025) This phenomenon is known as the global healthcare equity gap. We have the tools to save lives globally. However, without proper distribution systems, these tools cannot reach those in need. Real progress requires changes to the status quo. (carmen.suen, 2025)

The inverse care law is a principle that suggests that the availability of good medical care tends to vary inversely with the need of the population served. Therefore, the communities suffering the most from certain conditions do not have the same availability to treatment compared to other communities. (The Lancet, 2021) Recently, data published by the WHO has highlighted that global healthcare inequality is worsening and that children are 15 times more likely to die before reaching the age of 5 in poorer countries compared to wealthier countries. Also, modelling predicts that 1.8 million children could be saved by reducing global healthcare inequality. (WHO, 2025) This pattern is sustained by chronic underfunding of healthcare systems worldwide, alongside persistent workforce shortages. These challenges are compounded by insufficient infrastructure, both digital and physical. As a result, lower-income countries and regions are disproportionately affected. When these limitations result in unequal access to the newest and most advanced technologies, populations around the world most affected by certain diseases are unable to benefit from early diagnosis, innovative treatment and preventative care. (WHO, 2025)

As technology slowly becomes ever more integrated into our daily lives, it has an equally important place in advancing healthcare. With new automated technologies, services such as telemedicine, AI-driven diagnostics and personalized genomics all are slowly becoming a cornerstone of medicine. These innovations are revolutionising treatment and are leading to better patient care. (Thacharodi et al., 2024) However, these advancements are not equally felt by all around the world. For citizens who live in areas with the infrastructure and the ability to access the internet, services such as telemedicine enable health conditions to be picked up and treated early. However, for people who do not have access to these services they may be forced to wait until the condition becomes unmanageable before being able to access medical services. (Global strategy on digital health 2020-2027, n.d.) As higher income countries shift towards AI-integrated healthcare systems, low-income countries, some of whom struggle to get basic broadband country wide, are being excluded from this new global standard of care unable to replicate the necessary cloud based, data heavy infrastructure. (Worldbank.org, 2025)

Alongside a software barrier, physical infrastructure barriers further prevent the full benefits of the newest treatments to be felt by all. The “infrastructure bottleneck". For example, many of the most sophisticated treatments like mRNA vaccines now require a specialised cold chain without which they are rendered ineffective upon arrival. The cold chain market is predicted to grow by 10.8% in the coming two years highlighting how this technology is expected to become more prevalent. (P&S Intelligence, 2024) Cold chain requires extremely low temperature storage and precision handling. In areas with unstable power supplies or a lack of refrigerated logistics, these treatments cannot be delivered to those who need it. (Cold Chain Federation, n.d.) Wealthier nations can coordinate and fund “smart” pharmacies and ensure that their populations have access to these medications. This disparity ensures that poorer nations are trapped in a cycle of using older medication whereas the rest of the world is able to move towards higher tech, personalized cures. (Peters, n.d.) Without a foundation of a stable energy supply and digital infrastructure, the divide will only grow.

Critics argue that attempting to solve healthcare inequality is a luxury that we cannot afford when faced with immediate worldwide threats such as climate change or anti-microbial resistance. From this perspective, the focus globally should be shifted to the engineering of drought resistance crops or into research of new antibiotics. (Magnano San Lio et al., 2023) However, we cannot solve climate induced risks and track the next antimicrobial superbug without a baseline of well-connected healthcare systems around the world. If a new strain emerges in a region excluded from the global health grid, the next pandemic may remain invisible until it reaches epidemic or global scale. (World Health Organization, 2023) For climate-resilient health systems to become functional they must also reach the baseline of having adequate power and internet connection. (Low-Carbon and Resilience Strategies for the Health Sector, n.d.)

To solve the global healthcare equity gap the global community must shift from the traditional ways of building large resource heavy hospitals to act as hubs, to decentralised, AI supported community health hubs. The use of AI would be invaluable to healthcare workers. Screenings for diseases such as tuberculosis could occur at the clinic level, reducing the need for and strain on expensive hospital-based facilities. (Wheeler, 2025) Traditionally, the “last mile” of delivery in conflict prone and rural areas has proved challenging to navigate. Drone based technology offers an alternative to failing road infrastructure by providing an autonomous delivery system for life saving medication as seen in the Malawi drone testing corridor. (www.unicef.org, n.d.) Paired with the use of satellite link technology, this allows even the most remote of clinics to access global healthcare networks even with unstable internet connection. (Author, 2024) This combination allows for even the most remote clinics to request and receive medication that previously would have been inaccessible and for patient health records to be digitised to allow for better monitoring of global health trends.

However, investment in technology alone cannot close the gap without a radical policy shift. A move towards open-source medicine and mandatory patent sharing of life saving technologies is necessary. By creating patent pools similar to the medicine patent pool, but expanded to include mRNA and gene therapies, nations can produce medication locally. (MPP, n.d.) This helps lower costs, stabilise supply chains and ensures that modern innovation is not fully controlled by large pharmaceutical companies but accessible to those who need it globally. Investment in digital leapfrogging, drone and satellite link technology as well as policy change are all essential in ensuring that all communities across the world will have access to the resources that they require.

Data from the World Bank and the WHO prove that health equity is not a global act of charity, but it is a fundamental pillar of global health security. As long as millions of people worldwide are excluded from the global healthcare network, the entire world remains vulnerable to the next pandemic or the next drug-resistant superbug. Many of these communities are also particularly vulnerable, due to geography, to the effects of climate-induced disease and simply do not have the resources to handle it. We have the digital tools and logistics necessary to bridge this divide. As we look to the future, we must recognise that the greatest medicine we can offer the world is the refusal to allow geography to define human destiny.
